Abstract
We present a consolidated and self-contained research monograph on integer factorization
by fixed and programmed diffusion on Cayley graphs, probabilistic detection of multiplicative
relations, and bounded-buffer packet rendezvous. The complete mathematical source of the
initial fixed-walk manuscript is reproduced in the body, including spectral proofs, numbertheoretic reductions, collision and cutoff theory, and examples. The stronger programmed
dyadic kernel is proved exactly: hL (e) = 1/r + s(r − s)/(r4L ) with s = 2L mod r. We show
how any certified nonzero relation of an odd-order projection allows factor extraction for
a favorable base; analyze four-wise-independent birthday sampling; and study multiscale
rendezvous with constant queues, conditional on established randomized packet-routing
results. The final part gives an explicit analog/digital hardware ledger, model limitations,
falsifiable FPGA and thermodynamic-prototype milestones, and an independent benchmark
plan. Logarithmic mathematical depth is carefully distinguished from physical running
time, memory and interconnect cost. No hardware speedup or manufacturing feasibility
is asserted without synthesis and measurements. This is a research reference from which
separate mathematical and industrial submissions may later be derived.

This volume consolidates the complete mathematical development of the original fixed-walk
Cayley-diffusion manuscript, the strengthened exact programmed-kernel argument, the order-tofactor reduction, collision probability bounds, and the subsequent studies of tagged butterfly and
multiscale rendezvous networks. The historic mathematical text is retained rather than replaced
by short summaries; its original numerical projections are preserved and explicitly segregated
from later engineering estimates.
Three evidence levels are distinguished throughout: proved statements under stated hypotheses;
conditional consequences relying on an ideal parallel primitive or a classical external network
theorem; and engineering hypotheses awaiting simulation, synthesis, device characterization,
or measurements. In particular, logarithmic layer count is not logarithmic physical time and
neither implies a polynomial-size classical factoring circuit. No prototype is claimed to have been
fabricated.
The original source is preserved independently in source_original/main.tex so that a reader
may compare every mathematical argument with this reorganized presentation. Provenance of
the new sections is documented in the accompanying PROVENANCE.md.

or a commissioned Extropic design. The name on the title page is carried over from the research

Introduction

1.1

Motivation and scope

Factoring a composite integer N is a cornerstone problem in computational number theory and
cryptography, intimately tied to the structure of (Z/N Z)∗ ; see, e.g., [HW08, CP05, Me96, BS96].
Classical methods are highly nontrivial and often subexponential, but no polynomial-time
algorithm in log2 N is known in the standard digital model [CP05].
Shor’s algorithm shows that, in the quantum circuit model, factoring reduces to order finding
and order finding can be solved in polynomial time via quantum period finding/Fourier sampling
[Sh94, Sh97, NC10, EJ96]. For recent advances and generalizations see [CBK22, Hh25, Ra24,
Re25, XQLM23] and references therein. In broad terms: one encodes modular exponentiation
into a coherent linear evolution, extracts periodicity through interference, and completes the
computation by classical post-processing (continued fractions and verification) [Sh94, Sh97,
KSV99, NC10].
This paper investigates a different computational primitive: diffusion on a finite graph as an
analog mechanism for order finding. Specifically, we analyze an iterated half-lazy random
walk (discrete-time heat flow) on a weighted Cayley graph attached to a cyclic subgroup of
(Z/N Z)∗ . Our objective is not to improve on Shor’s algorithm within the usual digital/RAM or
quantum-circuit models. Rather, we propose a hybrid model in which standard digital arithmetic
is augmented by a diffusion process that can be iterated and queried. In this accounting,
order recovery is achieved using poly(log2 N ) digital work together with O((log2 N )2 ) diffusion
iterations, under explicit interface assumptions.

1.3

Physical diffusion primitive, resource scaling and locality

Our complexity bounds track two resources separately: digital steps and diffusion steps.
Digital steps. Deterministic operations on O(log2 N )-bit integers (modular arithmetic, gcd,
verification, bookkeeping), all polynomial in log2 N in the standard arithmetic/RAM sense
[Kn97, BS96].
6

Diffusion steps. One application of a fixed local-averaging operator, the half-lazy walk operator
W on a graph X, together with a prescribed readout of a small number of “temperature”
coordinates of a ℓ1 -normalized state vector.
The intended interpretation of a diffusion step is hardware-based. Indeed, the speedup occurs
only if diffusion is treated as a genuine hardware primitive: a single hardware-level update that
performs local averaging simultaneously at every vertex, whose wall-clock cost (the actual elapsed
real time as measured by a clock) does not scale with the number of vertices, and whose required
readout precision is achievable with poly(log2 N ) overhead. In this model, the wall-clock cost
of one diffusion step is treated as independent of the number of vertices represented, because
all vertices are updated in parallel. If diffusion is digitally simulated by explicitly updating a
length-r state vector (with r = ordN (b) possibly as large as φ(N ), the Euler totient function),
then the advantage disappears and the procedure becomes exponential in log2 N .
This assumption shifts cost from time to physical resources. Any literal diffuser must represent a
state over a vertex set of size Ω(|X|), so device area/energy scales (at least) linearly with |X|.
Our results are therefore conceptual and model-based: they show that, given such a diffusion
primitive with sufficient readout precision, the order r is determined from a single heat-kernel
value after O((log2 N )2 ) diffusion iterations.
A further operational point is locality. Although the underlying Cayley graph has r vertices, the
dynamics are specified by a short generating set and are local; thus an implementation need not
pre-construct the full graph. Instead, the diffusion mechanism can evolve the state by repeated
local averaging and (conceptually) “grow” only the portion of the graph that is actually reached
within the prescribed number of iterations, in the spirit of standard random-walk perspectives
on Cayley graphs [LPW09, Ch97].

A diffusion-assisted factoring algorithm

In this section, we describe diffusion assisted factorization algorithm. It receives as an input
a positive integer N with m ≥ 2 odd prime factors. The algorithm returns a divisor d of
N , with probability at least p(m) = 1 − 2−m (m + 1) for each choice of a random integer
a ∈ {1, 2, . . . , N − 1}.
Before we proceed with the pseudocode for the algorithm, let us state the following two remarks.
Remark 5.1. Algorithm 1 is probabilistic in the same sense as Shor’s factoring algorithm: a
single trial (i.e. one choice of a and the subsequent steps) may end in a restart (continue in
Algorithm 1; equivalently, FAIL for that particular a), for instance because the extracted square
root satisfies x ≡ ±1 (mod N ) and hence yields only a trivial gcd.
However, for composite N with at least two distinct prime factors, each independent trial has
a nonzero success probability depending only on the CRT parity pattern (cf. Proposition 3.6
and Lemma 3.2). Consequently, repeating the procedure independently drives the overall failure
probability very close to 0.
We will leave for elsewhere the problem of optimizing the probability p(m) of success. In that
regard, the methodology of [Za13] seems applicable.
Remark 5.2. In practice one can efficiently exclude the cases “N is prime” and “N is a prime
power” before invoking Algorithm 1. For primality testing there are fast randomized tests (e.g.
Miller–Rabin), deterministic polynomial-time algorithms (AKS), and practical certificate-based
methods (e.g. ECPP). To test whether N is a prime power, one may first perform a perfect-power
test (decide whether N = uk with k ≥ 2) and, if so, apply a primality test to the base u. See, for
example, [CP05].

Scope, conventions, and the fixed diffusion model

Let N > 1, let b ∈ (Z/N Z)× , and write G = ⟨b⟩, r = ordN (b). We use e = 1 (mod N ) for the
identity. The input size is n = ⌈log2 N ⌉. A modular multiplication is an operation on O(n)-bit
integers, not a unit-cost bit operation. We distinguish arithmetic work, parallel depth, stored
state, routing, and readout precision throughout.
The original diffusion idea is to prepare δe , mix on G, and read a coordinate approaching 1/r.
The revised question is whether the mixing schedule and the arithmetic post-processing can
be improved while exposing their physical costs. Our main result is the exact kernel identity
in Theorem 11.1. The arithmetic reduction and success probability in Section 12 are classical
facts proved here in the form needed for relation finding; we make no originality claim for those
reductions. Binary averaging and accelerated walks have substantial antecedents, including the
work of Chung, Diaconis, and Graham and of Dubail and Massoulié [2, 3]. Our explicit schedule
is analyzed directly, without appealing to a new general mixing phenomenon. Its precise novelty
relative to that literature requires a broader bibliographic assessment.
Discrete heat kernels on regular graphs provide a related spectral framework; explicit formulas
and return-distribution applications were developed in [1]. Here the cyclic structure permits
a different, elementary finite-product formula. General background on finite-group walks and
spectral mixing is available in [4], and on factorization in [5].
Shor’s algorithm works in a different resource model [6]. A classical array with one scalar per
group element uses r physical degrees of freedom. An O(log N )-qubit register does not provide
independently readable classical access to its N amplitudes. Counting the applications of a linear
operator in both settings is therefore insufficient for a complexity comparison.

11.4

A support bound and the meaning of the improvement

A product of L operators, each supported on at most k shifts, has support of size at most k L
when applied to a delta mass. If its distribution is within 1/(2r) of uniform in every coordinate,
every coordinate is positive. Therefore k L ≥ r, and
L ≥ logk r.
For our three-tap layers, logarithmic depth is thus optimal in order of magnitude within this
bounded-support positive-averaging model when r grows comparably to N . The lower bound
says nothing about the precision or hardware area required to attain it.

The distinction between routing, coalescence, and sorting

A tagged endpoint is a pair (Xi , i), where a public polynomial seed reconstructs Ei from i.
The verifier rejects any purported equality unless it checks both Xi = Xj and Ei ̸= Ej . Given

16.3

A one-register receiver rather than an in-network merger

The main new architectural choice is to use B = 4m receivers for a lane with m input packets.
A hash function h : ⟨b⟩ → {0, . . . , B − 1} determines the receiver of each endpoint Xi . At each
receiver, the first arriving packet’s pair (Xi , i) is retained. Every later incoming pair (Xj , j) is
compared with that first pair. If the keys match, the receiver reports (i, j, Xi ) to the verifier;
otherwise it discards the incoming packet. The verifier reconstructs Ei , Ej from their sampling
seed and checks Ei ̸= Ej and b|Ei −Ej | ≡ 1 (mod N ).
This receiver detects any isolated useful pair: indices i < j for which Xi = Xj , Ei ̸= Ej , and
no other packet has their hashed receiver address. It may miss collisions at busy receivers.
That is allowed: one correct relation suffices, and no claimed relation is accepted without exact
modular verification. Each receiver holds only one resident record and one input word (two
complete-record slots), regardless of the number of arrivals over time.

16.6

Reproducibility, open issues, and publication boundaries

The accompanying simulate_bounded_butterfly.py provides a finite-size illustrative storeand-forward schedule with queue capacity q = 2, one record per directed edge per cycle and a
single-word streaming receiver. It samples fully independent exponents (a stronger condition
than four-wise independence) in an abstract cyclic group represented by integer reduction modulo
r. It does not implement the Leighton–Maggs–Ranade–Rao scheduler and hence cannot validate
that scheduler’s finite-size constants. It also does not emulate thermal physics.
No claim is made of an asymptotic improvement over classical factoring algorithms: the number
of physically laid-out routers and the modular arithmetic work per trial can dominate. The novel
portion to submit to peer review is the particular isolated collision theorem plus its multiscale
composition and resource accounting, carefully distinguished from classical birthday paradoxes,
pre-existing constant-buffer packet routing [15, 16], and parallel collision search [10].
The next non-asymptotic milestone is a clocked RTL prototype with synthesis-reported area and
timing, a measured random-seed error bound, and a reproducible end-to-end benchmark. Only
after these gates are met should a device-scale forecast or an external hardware proposal quote
factorization throughput.

Implementation priorities and acceptance criteria

1. Reproduce the exact integer-array experiments at N = 21, 143, 299, with the construction

Research directions and limits of the present results

The strongest mathematical development is a constructive logarithmic-depth averaging schedule
with an exact finite-size error, an arithmetic stopping certificate, and an extension to finite
abelian subgroup cardinalities. The most immediate digital improvements are controlled binary
sampling, first-relation extraction, and restarting an unfavorable base without redundant gcd
stabilization.
Several further questions are concrete enough to guide subsequent work:
1. Determine the least routing and storage resources needed to implement the modular permutation sequence while retaining the programmed-depth benefit. A short algebraic description of
the routes is not a physical layout.
2. Optimize the radix and schedule subject to a fixed conductance, fan-in, switching-energy, and
wire-length budget. A lower layer count alone does not select the best physical design.
3. Develop an experimentally validated interval readout, with capacitance calibration and
correlated-noise accounting, that can satisfy Theorem 11.4 at increasing orbit sizes.
4. Analyze a specific shared distinguished-point transition rule with integer-exponent labels,
instead of transferring iid birthday estimates to a correlated process without proof.
5. Extend comparative software benchmarks to random balanced semiprimes and several size
ranges, reporting distributions, failed trials, arithmetic work, memory traffic, and energy.
Established factoring algorithms must remain the primary baselines.
The exact kernel does not remove the potentially exponential number of represented coordinates,
and the scalar readout retains an order-dependent precision requirement. Consequently the
present results establish improved depth in an explicitly strengthened diffusion model and
improved implementations of the relation strategy. They do not establish polynomial-resource
classical factoring, a competitive large-scale physical machine, or an advantage over quantum
computation.

Concrete prototype architecture and acceptance criteria

22.1

A falsifiable three-part machine

The proposed first device comprises (i) an optional thermodynamic bit or seed generator; (ii)
exact digital exponentiation with authenticated endpoint labels; and (iii) a digital rendezvous
fabric followed by an independent factor verifier. The complete device is a hybrid; no physical
heat flow is claimed to perform modular arithmetic for free. The testable physical question is
whether the stochastic source improves energy per verified relation and factor at fixed total
system cost.

75

22.2

Phase A: software golden model

Choose C = 256 or 1024 total logical samples and split them into power-of-two lanes. For each
lane specify the seed of the degree-three polynomial over F2K , every Ei , each modular endpoint,
its hash destination, its arrival cycle, and the validated relation. For small composite numbers
the model should cross-check factors against trial division and an independently implemented
Pollard–Brent baseline. The test suite must cover prime input, even input, perfect powers,
nonunits a, r = 1, repeated exponent words, false hash collisions, odd-order bases and hostile
queue patterns. All stochastic failures must be recorded, not silently discarded.

22.3

Phase B: synthesizable FPGA demonstration

Implement packet width w = ⌈log2 N ⌉ + ⌈log2 m⌉ plus metadata, one complete-word link per
edge per cycle, bounded queue capacity q ∈ {2, 4}, and one-resident-word receiver. Record logic
slices/LUTs, flip-flops, memory blocks, routing congestion, critical-path frequency, actual bytes
moved, startup latency, and joules per verified relation. An RTL simulator is not a placement
and routing report: both must be provided before clock-rate claims.

22.4

Phase C: probabilistic-hardware integration

At first replace only the seed/Bernoulli source by a measured thermodynamic primitive. Estimate
min-entropy and temporal correlation; if a randomness extractor is used, state its seed assumptions
and quantitative total-variation guarantee. Keep FPGA routing and modular arithmetic identical
to the digital control. Compare end-to-end energy, wall time and reproducibility against the
same apparatus with a purely digital random-number generator.

22.5

Interface with Extropic’s platforms

The publicly described XTR-0 platform couples sampling circuits with conventional control
hardware, and the advertised Z1 chip has a sparse, fixed-degree pbit graph rather than generalpurpose arithmetic routers. These descriptions are evidence of a plausible probabilistic input
interface, not confirmation that Extropic supports a programmable 100-bit butterfly or direct
realization of Bt [17]. No affiliation, partnership or component availability is assumed.

Why the logarithmic statement is not a full factoring complexity claim

The programmed heat kernel concerns a linear transformation of an unknown-length state vector.
A literal analog device may need N cells, nonlocal switch connections and analog readout precision
√
on the order of r−2 . The tagged alternative avoids this readout, but produces Θ( r) samples,
each with exact modular arithmetic, and requires packet comparison and routing. A C-sample
batch of log-depth therefore does not imply that the number of physical gates or the sequential
work of a fixed-size classical computer is polynomial in log N . These resource models must never
be conflated.

23.2

Questions remaining before submission

The mathematically interesting open direction is a small-constant, physically local, constantbuffer rendezvous design whose time, energy, area and interconnect are rigorously accounted
for. It may be preferable to use an explicit O(log2 C) Batcher network as the first demonstrator.
A thermodynamic advance requires showing how its stochastic dynamics implements a useful
operator or distribution more economically than the digital baseline, beyond replacing a negligible
seed cost.
